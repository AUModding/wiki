As a budding modder, you might wonder, "Why should I care about writing *good*
code if it works?"

Think of it like sharing a meal recipe you're really proud of. Would you
leave the instructions vague? Leave out steps? What if you wrote a step
so poorly that someone following it blindly accidentally burns their
kitchen down?

In this scenario, the kitchen is your user's PC. Computers will happily
waste time doing the unnecessary things you tell them to do. "Burning the
kitchen down" here means tanking the user's game to 4 FPS, or worse,
corrupting saves. Their CPU would be spiking, the RAM is crying tears of
blood and the cooling fans are starting to sound like a jet engine. Yikes!

There are exceptions to this, of course. You, for example. Your testers.
All of you *want* your PCs to blow up, so you know what went wrong to fix it.

You want to keep your users happy. The easiest way to achieve that is to
write good code.

Nothing's worse than trying to diagnose a bug that seems to evade any form of
observation like a quantum particle. In this part of the wiki, we will walk you
through the core principles of writing clean, optimised mods. Not only will the
user's PC be happy, but future-you will also thank you.

[terms]({getRelativeLocaleUrl("en", "resources/guides")}) before proceeding, as
they will be used often.
